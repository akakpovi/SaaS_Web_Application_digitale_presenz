DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting & Conformance) is an email authentication protocol that tells receiving mail servers what to do when an email claiming to be from your domain fails authentication checks. In plain terms: it stops criminals from sending emails that look like they come from your business.

Why DMARC matters for small businesses

Email spoofing — where an attacker sends emails that appear to come from your domain — is one of the most common forms of phishing. If a criminal sends fraudulent invoices, requests for bank transfers, or phishing links using your business email address, the damage to your reputation can be severe even though you did nothing wrong.

DMARC, combined with SPF and DKIM, creates a reliable authentication chain that protects your customers and your brand.

SPF, DKIM and DMARC: the three layers

  • SPF (Sender Policy Framework) — specifies which mail servers are authorised to send email from your domain.
  • DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) — adds a cryptographic signature to your emails so receiving servers can verify they have not been tampered with.
  • DMARC — ties SPF and DKIM together and tells receiving servers what to do if an email fails: accept it, quarantine it, or reject it outright.

A minimal DMARC record

A DMARC record is a TXT record in your DNS, typically added to _dmarc.yourdomain.com. A simple starting policy looks like this:

v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.com

Start with p=none (monitor only), then move to p=quarantine and eventually p=reject once you are confident your legitimate mail is passing authentication.
